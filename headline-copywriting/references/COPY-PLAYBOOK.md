# Landing page copy playbook

Audience: anyone writing the landing page, sales page or homepage for their own product. This playbook does not contain copy. It is the method a copywriter follows. Every rule names its source in parentheses. Short names: Shapiro, Dry, Wiebe, Schwartz, SB7, Bridge, PAS, Dunford, Hormozi, Corey (the coreyhaines31 marketing skill files, public at github.com/coreyhaines31/marketingskills). Where an example from one real product helps, it is labelled as an example. Everywhere else, [your reader] and [your product] stand for yours.

## 1. Do the research before any sentence

Wiebe's method comes first. Mine the exact words [your reader] uses about their own pain: job posts and forum threads, Reddit and Facebook groups, community comments, reviews of competing products, support tickets, sales call transcripts, your own customer chats. Read line by line, highlight repeated phrases, do not summarize while reading, and tag themes (Wiebe, Corey customer-research). Keep a file of the raw phrases with their sources. The right kind of material sounds like a person said it. Build the headline and section titles from these phrases, not from a model's idea of marketing. Test every candidate against Dry's rule that great copy reads like the customer wrote it.

Then decide the awareness level (Schwartz). Work out where [your reader] sits. If they are problem-aware and often solution-aware, they already know the category exists and may already use something in it, so the hero should not explain the category. It should name the specific pain that remains and the specific outcome, which is Schwartz's level 3 headline: the need, proof it can be met, and how. If your reader is less aware, the hero has to start earlier, with the problem or the desire.

Then settle positioning before writing (Dunford). Name the real alternatives [your reader] has today: a manual workaround, a general-purpose tool, a competitor, or doing nothing new. Pick the one or two unique attributes only [your product] has, the value those create, who cares most, and the category that makes the value obvious. The objection "isn't this just [the obvious alternative]" is a competitive-alternatives question. Answer it by saying which alternative you beat and on what, not by denying the similarity. Do not position against phantom competitors (Dunford).

## 2. Follow the standard structure

Shapiro is firm that the standard order beats clever order, because deviation adds confusion: hero, social proof, call to action, features and objections, repeat call to action, footer. Corey adds the middle: problem, solution and benefits (three to five), how it works (three or four steps), objection handling, final call to action with risk reversal. SB7 gives the same skeleton in story terms. A workable merge for your page:

1. Hero: headline, subhead, one primary call to action, a real product visual (Shapiro, Corey).
2. Proof strip right under the hero (Shapiro). If logos do not exist, use a concrete number or a real result. Never invent any (Corey, Dry).
3. The problem in the reader's words, short, as a story beat (SB7 problem layers, PAS, Story Spine). One section.
4. The mechanism: what [your product] actually does differently, shown, not described (Dry visualize, Shapiro feature images).
5. Three to six feature blocks, each with a blunt header, a short paragraph that answers one objection, and a real screenshot (Shapiro).
6. How it works in three steps (SB7 plan, Corey).
7. Objection section: the biggest fears, "isn't this just [the obvious alternative]", price, and whatever your reader worries about losing control of (Shapiro, Dunford).
8. The founder or origin story, brief (Bridge). See section 5.
9. Final call to action that repeats the hero promise (Shapiro, SB7).

One idea per section, and each section should advance one argument (Corey, Wiebe's Rule of One).

## 3. Headline, subhead, call to action

Headline rules:
- It must be self-descriptive. Cover everything but the headline and a stranger should still know what you sell and for whom (Shapiro, Corey, SB7 cocktail party test).
- It makes one promise to one reader (Rule of One in Wiebe).
- Specific beats generic. Replace "get results faster" with a number, a timeframe or a thing someone can picture (Shapiro hook A, Dry).
- Run the "Now you can" test and the discomfort, vision, path shape from Corey's copy-frameworks file. Pick a formula from that file only as a starting point and fill every slot with a real specific.
- Optionally use Shapiro's hook B: answer the single biggest objection inside the header. Pick the strongest objection from your research and use only one.
- A rhetorical question that names the pain is acceptable if it is in the reader's voice (Corey), not if it labels your own claim ("The result?").

Subhead rules:
- One or two sentences. Sentence one says what the product is. Sentence two says how the headline claim is possible: the mechanism (Shapiro).
- No essay, no stacked adjectives.

Call to action rules:
- The button completes the headline's promise in a verb plus the thing they get (Shapiro, Corey). Avoid Submit, Learn More, Get Started with nothing attached.
- Repeat it after the objection section and at the bottom (Shapiro).
- State a real absence once near the button ("No card required") and only if true (Corey).

## 4. Objections and proof

- Collect the real objections from customers: "what nearly stopped you" (Shapiro). Put the biggest in the hero, the rest in the feature paragraphs and an objection section.
- Handle [the main objection] with evidence the reader can check, not reassurance. A before and after, a stated number, a check described step by step. Apply Dry's falsify test: "high quality" cannot be wrong, a specific claim that anyone could verify can.
- Handle "isn't this just [the obvious alternative]" in Dunford's terms: concede what is the same, name the status quo you replace, state the attribute only you have, and show it.
- Use Hormozi's equation as a per-section diagnostic. Dream outcome: what [your reader] ultimately wants. Perceived likelihood: proof it will work for them. Time delay: how fast they get it. Effort and sacrifice: how little they have to do or give up. Ask of every block which term it moves.
- Social proof should be specific and attributed. Testimonial text can be used as body copy (Dry). No fabricated stats or testimonials (Corey).

## 5. How to use story on a landing page

Do not turn the whole page into a story. Use story in three small, defined places, because Shapiro and Corey both lean on clarity and structure, while SB7 and Bridge lean on narrative.

- The reader is the hero (SB7). [Your reader] wants something, meets a problem (external: the visible obstacle; internal: how it makes them feel; philosophical: why it should not be this way), meets [your product] as the guide with empathy and authority, gets a three step plan, and avoids failure or reaches success. Map each page section to one of these seven parts and the page has a spine.
- Problem section as a Story Spine paragraph. Once a normal routine, one day something goes wrong for the reader, because of that more work, because of that a cost they feel, until finally. Keep it to a few lines in plain words. Agitate (PAS) only as far as the reader nods, since over-agitating reads as cringe (Corey, PAS notes).
- Founder story as a short Epiphany Bridge: where the founder was, the wall, the epiphany, what was built, what changed. Place it low on the page, never in the hero. Its job is belief that the same epiphany will work for the reader.

Where sources disagree: SB7 says the brand is never the hero, Bridge puts the founder's journey on stage. Resolve it by keeping the founder story short and ending it on the customer's result. Also, Shapiro says stay with the standard structure, SB7 and Bridge push narrative order. Use the standard order with narrative inside sections.

## 6. Voice and style

- Simple over complex, specific over vague, active over passive, confident over qualified, show over tell (Corey).
- Customer language over company language (Wiebe, Corey).
- Remove the model's default tells. Corey's ai-tells file bans contrast reveals ("It's not X, it's Y"), negation lists, trailing pile-ons, self-answered questions ("The result?"), stock openers ("Say goodbye to", "unlock"), and em dashes in short copy. Allow at most one fragment and one list of three per section. Swapping synonyms makes new tells, so fix from the facts.
- Swap test: if a line works unchanged on a competitor's site, rewrite it (Corey, Dry rule 3). If the differentiator is missing, flag it as a gap rather than filling it with adjectives.
- Flowing, plain sentences. Dry's constraint exercise is useful: write a line with no adjectives, then add back only the ones that carry fact.

## 7. Pre-publish checklist

Run every test. Fail any one and revise.

1. Five-second clarity (Shapiro, Wiebe uses FiveSecondTest): show the hero to someone outside the market for five seconds. They must say what it is and who it is for. Also show it to someone who is [your reader] and ask whether it is different from what they already use.
2. Header alone (Shapiro): cover everything but the headline. Is it enough?
3. Visualize (Dry): circle every abstract word (workflow, seamless, powerful, solution, platform) and replace with a thing a person could see or a number.
4. Falsify (Dry): every claim could be proven wrong by a check. If not, rewrite or cut. Every number is real and sourced.
5. Nobody else (Dry, Corey swap test, Dunford): paste each headline and section title into a competitor's page mentally. It must not fit.
6. One reader, one promise, one action (Wiebe Rule of One, Shapiro): each section has a single idea, and the page has a single primary call to action.
7. Customer voice (Wiebe): at least the hero and problem section reuse phrases lifted from real [reader] language, with sources noted in a file.
8. Awareness fit (Schwartz): the hero matches your reader's actual awareness level and does not explain what they already know.
9. Hormozi pass: for each section, name which of the four terms it moves. If none, cut it.
10. Read it aloud: no sentence you would not say to [your reader] across a table. Sentences over 20 words get read twice (Corey).
11. AI tells (Corey ai-tells): search for the banned patterns, em dashes included.
12. Six outsider questions (Shapiro): conversion readiness, interest 1 to 10, what is unclear, what to expand, what to cut by half, what sounds like marketing. Run them with one outsider and one person from [your reader] group.
13. Cocktail party test (SB7): the one-liner can be repeated after hearing it once.

## 8. Where the sources disagree or are thin

- Clever versus clear: Dry and Bridge reward vivid, surprising lines, Shapiro and Corey say choose clear over creative. Take both: clear on structure and headline, vivid in the concrete detail.
- Agitation: PAS says make the pain felt, Corey warns about cringe and AI tells, Copyblogger says target the dominant emotion, not the surface problem. The dominant pain is often an identity or status feeling rather than the surface failure. Verify in the research step.
- Story: see section 5.
- Standard structure versus differentiation: Shapiro is strict, Dunford implies category choice matters more than layout. Structure is standard, positioning is not.
- Evidence quality: Shapiro and Dunford were fetched from primary pages. Schwartz, Hormozi, Brunson, Rule of One and Pixar's spine are secondary summaries. Verify any exact quote before using it in print.
- Gap: this playbook rests on written sources, not on a fresh teardown of strong pages in your own market. Do a second pass by picking three to five well-regarded landing pages from products like yours (Linear, Stripe, Basecamp, Pitch, Figma, Gumroad are common candidates) and take actual screenshots, so you can compare structure and headline shape against your draft.
